# Cove Gap — Lore Introduction & Mythos Outline

## Framing Thesis

Cove Gap is not haunted. It is *occupied*. The woods around it were old before there were words for old, and what lives in them does not hate the town — it barely notices it, the way a mountain doesn't notice the moss on one particular stone. That indifference is the engine of the horror here: not malevolence, but scale. The settlers came to build a righteous life in what they read as empty wilderness. It was never empty. It was just patient.

This is the tonal needle to thread throughout: **dread from scale and unknowability, not from villainy.** The being doesn't hunt the town because it's cruel. It eats because it has always eaten, and it is very old, and the town is, geologically speaking, a very new and very small thing standing in its way.

## 1. The Founding

A congregation — called **The Brotherhood of Light** — left a more settled part of the country seeking land remote enough that no one would interfere with how they worshipped and governed themselves. Eastern Kentucky's folded, hard-to-reach hollows were perfect for that: isolation as a feature, not a bug. They picked a cove — a bowl of flat bottomland ringed by ridges — because it was defensible, arable, and hidden.

The Brotherhood chose the location of their town based on the fertility of the area and the protective nature of the surrounding geography, but the thing that drew them to this exact spot was their discovery of a large clearing with a massive tree in the middle, and a beautiful spring feeding a clear pond. They assumed that all these things were a good omen, but they did not realize that they were intruding on an ancient place that was beyond their understanding.

The Brotherhood has strict beliefs that are very rigid, but we don't want to define any specific theology for them so as nto to offend any group.

## 2. The Core Mythos: "The Dark Green"

Resist calling this a pantheon of "gods" in the Greek sense — no thrones, no court, no clean anthropomorphized personalities. Appalachian-cosmic-horror works better as **the forest itself having ancient, semi-discrete regions of awareness** — vast old presences that are *of* the land rather than ruling *over* it. A useful mental model: not gods who live in the woods, but the woods occasionally *waking up enough in one place* to be a god for a while.

Suggested framework — **Aspects**, not species. Every ancient entity embodies one elemental truth of the wilderness, scaled up past reason:

- **Hunger** — consumption without malice. Your thousand-year boar fits here: not a monster that wants to hurt you, an appetite older than ethics.
- **Rot / Return** — decay as a force, not a tragedy. Things that unmake, patiently, because unmaking is also part of growth.
- **Growth / Root** — the woods reclaiming, spreading, absorbing. Slow horror — a barn overtaken in one season that should have taken twenty.
- **Water / Hollow** — what's in the deep coves, the caves, the places light doesn't reach. Stillness, pressure, drowning-that-isn't-water.
- **Stone / Watch** — the ridgelines themselves, old and unmoving, that seem to observe. The horror of being *seen* by something that has never once needed to move.

Each Aspect can have one or more named ancient entities as its expression in this specific place — you don't need a full pantheon, even 4-6 well-developed entities (one primary "big" one, a few lesser ones) will carry a whole game.

### Example entity, fleshed out from your boar idea:

**The Hollow Boar** (placeholder name — consider something that sounds like folk-name-turned-legend, the way real Appalachian folklore names things: *the Sow of Widow's Ridge*, *Old Bristle*, *the Rooting Dark*)
- Aspect: Hunger
- Not a "boss monster" in the traditional sense — more a **weather system**. It doesn't have a lair you raid; it has a *range*, and it moves through it on its own ancient logic. Sightings are omens, not encounters, until the game decides you're ready for the encounter.
- Scale should be conveyed indirectly before it's ever conveyed directly: trees rubbed raw eight feet up the trunk, a wallow the size of a house foundation, a season where every root cellar in a quarter mile got broken into and *eaten clean*. Let players piece together its size from evidence before they ever see it.
- Its horror isn't teeth, it's **scale + indifference** — the realization that something this large has been *quietly nearby the whole time* and simply had no reason to care.

## 3. Design Principles for Existential (Not Jump-Scare) Horror

Concrete rules of thumb to keep every future entity/lore beat consistent with the tone:

1. **Bigness over teeth.** Awe first, danger second. The scariest fact about most of these things should be their scale or age, not their claws.
2. **Indifference over malice.** These entities rarely target the town *because* it's the town. They target it because it's in the way, in the range, or made a sound. This is scarier than hatred because it can't be reasoned with or appeased in a normal sense.
3. **Evidence before encounter.** Let the investigation loop do the heavy lifting — tracks, wreckage, half-remembered settler journal fragments, animals gone silent. The reveal should almost always be smaller than what the player already imagined, or exactly as bad, never a cheap jump.
4. **Faith as a real but limited tool.** The settlers' religion shouldn't be flatly "wrong" or flatly "the answer" — more interesting if it's a genuine partial ward (certain rites/wards actually work, mechanically) that also fundamentally misunderstands what it's warding against. That tension (right practice, wrong theology) is rich, human, and avoids both cheap debunking and cheap validation.
5. **The forest is not evil, the forest is old.** Avoid demon/devil framing entirely if you can. These aren't fallen or corrupted things. They predate the moral categories the settlers would use to judge them. That's the existential part — the terror of a universe that isn't hostile, just vastly unconcerned.
6. **Named, not classified.** Prefer folk names over taxonomic ones in-game text ("the Sow of Widow's Ridge," not "Ancient Forest Boar Tier 3"). Bestiary UI can hold the mechanical name; in-fiction text should always sound like something a scared settler would actually call it.

## 4. A Careful Note on the "Ancient Appalachian Gods" Framing

Real Appalachian folklore and the mythologies of the Indigenous peoples of the region (Cherokee, Shawnee, and others) are living traditions, not a public-domain aesthetic to lift wholesale — worth treating as inspiration for *tone and texture* (oral folk-naming conventions, land-as-character, omens-and-signs storytelling) rather than borrowing specific real deities, spirits, or sacred stories directly. Inventing your own wholly fictional Aspects/entities (as above) gets you the flavor you want without that problem, and honestly gives you more creative freedom since you're not constrained by an existing canon.
